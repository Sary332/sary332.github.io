Over half of Flowdesk's cancellations followed a failed payment.

Three teams blamed three different causes for rising churn. I tested each one against the same data in SQL. One held up.

A solo project in SQL Server on simulated subscription data, September 2024 to August 2026.

460 subscriptions cancelled between September 2024 and August 2026. Press a button to see which ones followed a failed payment.

One dot is one cancelled subscription. Strict definition: a payment failed in the 30 days before the cancel date and no successful payment followed.

Three theories, one test

Each explanation got the same treatment: a rate, a 95% confidence interval, and no counting of customers who were too new to judge. Shaded bars are the intervals. Where they do not overlap, the difference is real.

Revenue was shrinking faster than the customer count

Until mid-2025, logo churn and revenue churn told the same story. From August 2025, revenue churn ran higher every month and the gap kept widening.

Plan downgrades explain part of it, and they are invisible if you only count customers who leave. There were 2 downgrades in the five months before August 2025. In the five months after: 2, 5, 5, 10, and 10. Of 86 downgrades overall, 79% were Growth to Starter.

Downgrades are not the whole story. Cancellations by high-tier customers still cost the most revenue in almost every month.

How I tested it

1

Clean the six tables

Removed 11 duplicate payments, unified channel names that differed only by case and spacing, and rebuilt six missing cancel dates from the events table.

2

Define "active" by date

A status column only holds today's answer. Counting by start and end dates keeps every past month accurate.

3

Test each theory fairly

A 95% interval on every comparison. Payments and signups too recent to have had 30 or 90 days to cancel are left out, so they are not mistaken for loyal customers.

4

Rank the evidence

Order the theories by strength, recommend one area to fix, and list what still needs confirming with stakeholders.

The core of the payment test, from the project's SQL script

-- Did the subscription cancel within 30 days of this payment?

SELECT status,
       COUNT(*)                         AS n,
       SUM(cancel_within_30d)           AS cancel_30d,
       AVG(cancel_within_30d * 1.0)     AS rate
FROM   payment_outcome
GROUP BY status;

-- payment_outcome flags each payment:

CASE WHEN effective_end_date > payment_date
      AND effective_end_date <= DATEADD(DAY, 30, payment_date)
     THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS cancel_within_30d

If I could fix one thing

Rebuild payment retries and dunning

Retry failed cards automatically and warn customers before they lapse. It touches the biggest share of churn, the fix is well understood in SaaS, and the result is easy to measure: compare involuntary churn before and after.

High impact, medium effort. Owner: Finance with Product.

Fix onboarding for Paid Ads customers on Starter

A smaller second step if there is room. The effect is real but limited to one channel on one tier.

Medium impact, low effort. Owner: Growth.

Report revenue churn next to logo churn

Show both every month so downgrades stop hiding behind a steady customer count.

Medium impact, low effort. Owner: Analytics.

What it can't tell you

The data shows that failed payments predict churn, not why they failed. Expired cards, empty balances, and gateway problems need different fixes, and that detail sits with the payment provider.

Historical revenue figures use each subscription's current plan. The worst-case distortion is about 15.7%, which I judged too small to justify a full rebuild.

"Involuntary churn" here means a failed payment that never recovered before cancelling. Finance should confirm that definition before the number goes into a report.

All data is simulated for this portfolio. The methods carry over; the figures do not describe a real company.
