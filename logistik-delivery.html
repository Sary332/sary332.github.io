Bandung had five drivers for 1,076 orders, and 59% of its deliveries missed the SLA.

Kirim.in, a courier startup, kept hearing merchant complaints about late deliveries and could not say if the cause was a city, the drivers, or unrealistic contracts. I answered it in SQL Server.

A solo project in T-SQL on simulated data from four cities, November 2025 to August 2026. Late means slower than the SLA hours in the merchant's contract, counted from pickup to delivery.

Was my first number right?

Share of delivered orders that missed the merchant's SLA, under two clocks

81% became 30%

My first query started the clock at the order date, but the contract SLA counts from pickup. That folded Kirim.in's own waiting time into the delivery time. Fixed, the breach rate fell from 81.33% to 29.85%, and Bandung was the outlier under both clocks.

Not one answer

Measured against the promised date, only 3.2% of deliveries were late, not 29.9%. For cities the answer holds: Bandung is 7.9% against 1.7% to 2.7% elsewhere. For merchants it does not. TokoBaru looks worst only because I had to fill in its missing dates. Without those rows, Kirana Furniture leads at 6.6%.

What to do first

1

Add drivers in Bandung

Bandung has 5 drivers for 1,076 orders, 215 each, against 104 to 134 in the other cities. Three more drivers would bring it to about 134, Jakarta's level. That is arithmetic, not a forecast. This data cannot show why hiring there lagged.

2

Keep heavy parcels off motorbikes

Motorbike attempts above 7 kg failed 20% of the time, 78 of 385, against 5.5% for vans on parcels that heavy. At the van rate, about 57 fewer attempts would have failed. That assumes vans have spare room, which this data cannot show.

3

Report late two ways, and fix TokoBaru's feed

Report the SLA breach rate from pickup for operations, and the promised-date rate for customers. TokoBaru Fashion's system never sent promised dates, so until it does, that merchant cannot be judged fairly.

The query behind the fix

This project has no dashboard. The work is in the SQL, so here is the query that corrected my first mistake. It starts the clock at pickup, which is what the contract SLA means.

WITH delivered AS (
    SELECT o.customer_city, m.sla_hours,
        CAST(DATEDIFF(MINUTE, d.pickup_time, d.delivery_time) AS FLOAT) / 60.0 AS actual_hours
    FROM dbo.deliveries d
    JOIN dbo.orders o    ON o.order_id    = d.order_id
    JOIN dbo.merchants m ON m.merchant_id = o.merchant_id
    WHERE d.delivery_status = 'Delivered'
)
SELECT customer_city,
    COUNT(*) AS delivered_count,
    CAST(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN actual_hours > sla_hours THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
         / COUNT(*) AS DECIMAL(5,2)) AS pct_sla_breach
FROM delivered
GROUP BY customer_city
ORDER BY pct_sla_breach DESC;

Simplified from section 7.2 of the script. The full file also deduplicates with ROW_NUMBER, finds hidden spelling variants with COLLATE Latin1_General_BIN, and flags every backfilled row so later checks can leave it out.

How I built it

1

Load and link

Four tables (merchants, drivers, orders, deliveries) loaded with BULK INSERT. That skips constraint checks, so I re-checked every foreign key by hand afterwards.

2

Clean the planted mess

Removed 69 duplicate delivery rows, folded 13 city spellings into 4, and flipped 51 negative weights back. 63 zero weights became NULL, and 538 missing promised dates were filled with order date plus SLA and flagged.

3

Answer six questions

City speed, SLA breaches, driver failures by vehicle and weight, merchants, two definitions of late, and where to add drivers. Failures are counted per delivery attempt, since one order can have two.

4

Check my own work

Caught the order-date clock mistake myself, then tested the TokoBaru result with and without the rows I filled in. Each figure was also compared with a reference answer guide.

What it can't tell you

The data shows that Bandung is short of drivers. It does not show why.

Why Bandung hired slower than its orders grew is outside this data. That needs input from HR or operations.

TokoBaru's promised dates are my assumption, not recorded data. They are flagged and tested, but the merchant stays unjudged under the promised-date view.

Driver IDs are missing for 490 deliveries before January 2026, so the driver and vehicle findings rest on 5,298 of 5,788 attempts.

Orders per driver assumes each driver works in the city where they are registered. That holds for 96% to 98% of deliveries, so it is a fair but not perfect approximation.

All data is simulated for this portfolio, with problems planted on purpose. The methods carry over; the figures do not describe a real company.
